Unit I: Propositional Logic CO1

Fundamental Logic Operators

Connectives establish truth-functional relationships between propositions. Master implications (\(p \rightarrow q\)) and biconditionals (\(p \leftrightarrow q\)), as they form the bedrock of mathematical proofs.

Example: "If it rains (p), then the ground is wet (q)." \(p \rightarrow q\).

Building on our understanding of Fundamental Logic Operators, let's dive into Logical Equivalences.

Logical Equivalences

De Morgan's Laws (\(\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q\)) and Implication Equivalence (\(p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg p \lor q\)) are pivotal for simplifying compound propositions without brute-force truth tables.

Example: "It's false that I am cold AND tired" equals "I am not cold OR I am not tired."

Moving forward from Logical Equivalences, let's break down the mechanics of Predicates & Quantifiers.

Predicates & Quantifiers

Examiner Trick: Nested Quantifiers. Remember that \(\forall x \exists y\) is NOT equivalent to \(\exists y \forall x\). The order dictates dependency!

Universal (\(\forall\)) implies all elements in the domain; Existential (\(\exists\)) asserts at least one exists. Negating them flips the quantifier.

Example: \(\neg(\forall x P(x)) \equiv \exists x \neg P(x)\).

Moving forward from Predicates & Quantifiers, let's break down the mechanics of Rules of Inference.

Rules of Inference

Modus Ponens (\(p, p \rightarrow q \vdash q\)) and Modus Tollens (\(\neg q, p \rightarrow q \vdash \neg p\)) validate arguments syntactically.

Niche Topic: Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent. Given \(p \rightarrow q\) and \(q\), concluding \(p\) is invalid.

Example: If it rains, ground is wet. Ground is wet. Therefore, it rained (Invalid!).

Having established the core principles of Rules of Inference, we now turn our attention to Symbolic Translations (Propositional Logic) (Applied Concept).

Symbolic Translations (Propositional Logic)Applied Concept

Understanding how to convert English to symbolic logic and vice-versa.

Example: "It is not raining but I wear a raincoat" translates to \(\neg p \land r\). (Note: 'but' translates to logical AND).

Example: \((p \lor q) \land \neg r\): "It is raining or I carry an umbrella, and I do not wear a raincoat."

Having established the core principles of Symbolic Translations (Propositional Logic) (Applied Concept), we now turn our attention to Specific Truth Tables (Applied Concept).

Specific Truth TablesApplied Concept

Recognizing Tautologies directly from truth tables.

Example: \((p \rightarrow q) \lor (q \rightarrow p)\) evaluates to True in all cases (Tautology).

Example: \((p \rightarrow q) \leftrightarrow (\neg q \rightarrow \neg p)\) is also a Tautology, illustrating the contrapositive equivalence.

Transitioning from Specific Truth Tables (Applied Concept), we now examine Conditional Statements Truth Values (Applied Concept), which builds upon these foundational rules.

Conditional Statements Truth ValuesApplied Concept

A conditional statement (\(p \rightarrow q\)) is false only when \(p\) is True and \(q\) is False.

Example: "If 1 + 1 = 3, then 2 + 2 = 5" is True (False \(\rightarrow\) False).

Example: "If 1 + 1 = 2, then dogs can fly" is False (True \(\rightarrow\) False).

With Conditional Statements Truth Values (Applied Concept) in mind, we can see why Permutations & Equations (Applied Concept) becomes crucial in this context.

Permutations & EquationsApplied Concept

Applying factorial definitions to solve for unknowns.

Example: Solving \((n + 1)P_3 = 5 \times nP_2\). Expanding gives \(\frac{(n+1)!}{(n-2)!} = 5 \times \frac{n!}{(n-2)!}\). Canceling \((n-2)!\) and \(n!\) leaves \(n + 1 = 5 \implies n = 4\).

Unit II: Counting Methods CO2

Pigeonhole Principle

If \(N\) items are placed into \(k\) boxes, at least one box contains \(\lceil N/k \rceil\) items. Crucial for existence proofs without direct construction.

Example: Among 13 people, at least 2 were born in the same month.

To fully appreciate Combinatorics: Stars and Bars, we must apply what we just learned about Pigeonhole Principle.

Combinatorics: Stars and Bars

Niche Topic: Stars & Bars (Multiset Coefficients). Finds non-negative integer solutions to \(x_1 + x_2 + ... + x_k = n\).

Formula is \(C(n + k - 1, k - 1)\). Applies when items are indistinguishable but bins are distinct.

Example: Distributing 10 identical candies among 3 children: \(C(10+3-1, 3-1) = 66\).

Having established the core principles of Combinatorics: Stars and Bars, we now turn our attention to Linear Recurrence Relations.

Linear Recurrence Relations

Characterize sequences via previous terms. Solve \(a_n = c_1a_{n-1} + c_2a_{n-2}\) using the characteristic equation \(r^2 - c_1r - c_2 = 0\).

Example: Fibonacci \(a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}\) gives roots \((1 \pm \sqrt{5})/2\).

To fully appreciate Generating Functions, we must apply what we just learned about Linear Recurrence Relations.

Generating Functions

Examiner Trick: Using partial fraction decomposition to extract coefficients from \(G(x) = \sum a_n x^n\).

Translates sequence convolution into polynomial multiplication, elegantly solving non-homogeneous recurrences.

Example: Sequence 1, 1, 1... corresponds to \(1/(1-x)\).

Transitioning from Generating Functions, we now examine Solving Generating Functions (Applied Concept), which builds upon these foundational rules.

Solving Generating FunctionsApplied Concept

Mapping generating functions back to sequences.

Example: The generating function \(G(x) = \frac{1}{1 - 2x}\) takes the form \(\frac{1}{1 - rx}\), which corresponds to the sequence \(a_n = r^n\). Here, \(r = 2\), so the solution is \(a_n = 2^n\).

To fully appreciate Applied Recurrence Relations (Applied Concept), we must apply what we just learned about Solving Generating Functions (Applied Concept).

Applied Recurrence RelationsApplied Concept

Solving specific forms and initial value problems.

Example: \(a_n = 10a_{n-1} - 25a_{n-2}\). Characteristic equation: \(r^2 - 10r + 25 = 0 \implies (r - 5)^2 = 0\). Root is \(r = 5\) (repeated). Form: \(a_n = (c_1 + c_2n)5^n\).

Example: Non-linear relation \(a_n = a_{n-1}^2\). If \(a_1 = 2\), then \(a_2 = 4, a_3 = 16\).

Unit III: Graph Theory CO3

Handshaking Lemma

The sum of degrees of all vertices equals \(2|E|\). A foundational invariant proving odd-degree vertices must occur in pairs.

Example: A graph with 5 vertices of degree 3 cannot exist since \(15 \neq 2|E|\).

Moving forward from Handshaking Lemma, let's break down the mechanics of Euler & Hamilton Paths.

Euler & Hamilton Paths

Examiner Trick: Euler traverses all edges exactly once; Hamilton traverses all vertices exactly once. Do not conflate them!

Euler circuits require all vertices to have an even degree. Hamiltonian has no simple definitive check (NP-complete).

Example: \(K_3\) has both paths, but \(K_{2,3}\) is not Hamiltonian.

While Euler & Hamilton Paths gives us the theoretical background, Graph Isomorphism provides the next layer of complexity.

Graph Isomorphism

Two graphs are isomorphic if a bijection preserves adjacency. Invariants: vertex count, edge count, and degree sequence.

Example: A square and a 4-cycle drawn as a figure-eight are isomorphic.

Now that we have a solid grasp of Graph Isomorphism, the natural next step is to explore Bipartite Graphs & Matching.

Bipartite Graphs & Matching

Niche Topic: Hall's Marriage Theorem. A bipartite graph \(G=(V_1, V_2, E)\) has a complete matching for \(V_1\) iff \(|N(S)| \ge |S|\) for all \(S \subseteq V_1\).

A graph is bipartite if and only if it contains no odd-length cycles.

Example: Assigning 3 workers to 3 tasks optimally without overlap.

Unit IV: Set Theory & Relations CO4

Set Operations & Power Sets

Operations include union, intersection, difference, and symmetric difference (\(\oplus\)). The power set \(\mathcal{P}(S)\) contains all subsets.

Example: If \(S = \{1, 2\}\), \(\mathcal{P}(S) = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2\}, \{1, 2\}\}\). Size is \(2^n\).

Transitioning from Set Operations & Power Sets, we now examine Equivalence Relations, which builds upon these foundational rules.

Equivalence Relations

A relation must be reflexive (\(xRx\)), symmetric (\(xRy \implies yRx\)), and transitive (\(xRy \land yRz \implies xRz\)). Forms equivalence classes.

Example: Congruence modulo \(n\), where \(a \equiv b \pmod n\) divides \(\mathbb{Z}\) into \(n\) classes.

With Equivalence Relations in mind, we can see why Partial Orderings (Posets) becomes crucial in this context.

Partial Orderings (Posets)

Examiner Trick: Hasse Diagrams. Ensure you remove reflexive loops and transitive edges to depict minimal/maximal elements accurately.

Requires reflexivity, antisymmetry (\(xRy \land yRx \implies x=y\)), and transitivity.

Example: The "divides" relation on integers forms a poset, visualizable via Hasse.

graph BT 1 --> 2 1 --> 3 2 --> 6 3 --> 6
Building on our understanding of Partial Orderings (Posets), let's dive into Injective, Surjective, Bijective.

Injective, Surjective, Bijective

Niche Topic: Cardinality mappings. If a bijection exists between two sets, they share the same cardinality (\(\aleph_0\) for countably infinite).

Injective (1-to-1): no two \(x\) map to same \(y\). Surjective (onto): every \(y\) is mapped. Bijective: both, thus invertible.

Example: \(f(x) = 2x\) from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\) is bijective.

Unit V: Trees & Automata CO5

Tree Properties

A tree is a connected, undirected graph with no simple circuits. It inherently possesses \(n-1\) edges for \(n\) vertices.

Example: A hierarchical file system directory structure.

Having established the core principles of Tree Properties, we now turn our attention to Spanning Trees.

Spanning Trees

Examiner Trick: Kruskal's vs Prim's. Both find Minimum Spanning Trees, but Kruskal sorts edges globally while Prim expands locally from a node.

A subgraph that contains all vertices and is a tree. Critical for optimizing network routing with minimal cost.

Example: Connecting 5 cities with the least amount of copper wire.

Now that we have a solid grasp of Spanning Trees, the natural next step is to explore Finite State Machines (FSMs).

Finite State Machines (FSMs)

Niche Topic: Pumping Lemma for Regular Languages. Used exclusively to prove a language is NOT regular by demonstrating string repetition violations.

DFAs have exactly one transition per state-symbol pair. NFAs allow multiple. They are computationally equivalent in expressive power.

Example: A vending machine dispensing a soda only after exactly 50 cents is inserted.

With Finite State Machines (FSMs) in mind, we can see why Boolean Algebra becomes crucial in this context.

Boolean Algebra

A distributive lattice representing logical operations. Simplification uses consensus theorem and Karnaugh maps to minimize circuit gates.

Example: \(AB + AB' = A(B + B') = A\).
