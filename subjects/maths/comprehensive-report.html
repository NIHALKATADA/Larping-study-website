Unit I: Propositional Logic and Equivalences

Propositions and Translations

  • Proposition: A declarative sentence that is either True or False, but not both. Examples of propositions: 'Every prime number greater than 2 is odd' (False), '7 + 8 = 16' (False), 'The Moon is a planet' (False), 'India is located in Asia' (True).
  • Logical Connectives: AND ($\wedge$), OR ($\vee$), XOR ($\oplus$), NOT ($\neg$), Conditional ($\rightarrow$), Biconditional ($\leftrightarrow$).
  • Translating English to Symbolic: Break sentences into simple propositions ($p, q, r$). Example: 'Either it is below freezing or it is snowing, but it is not snowing if it is below freezing' translates to $(p \vee q) \wedge (p \rightarrow \neg q)$.
  • Translating Symbolic to English: Combine simple phrases using connective terms ('and', 'or', 'if...then', 'if and only if').
Exam Trap: Questions like 'What time is it?' or 'x + 1 = 2' are NOT propositions because they don't have a fixed truth value.
Moving forward from Propositions and Translations, let's break down the mechanics of Negation, Converse, Inverse, and Contrapositive.

Negation, Converse, Inverse, and Contrapositive

  • Negation ($\neg p$): The opposite truth value. Example: 'The classroom is empty' becomes 'The classroom is not empty'.
  • Conditional Statement ($p \rightarrow q$): 'If $p$, then $q$'. It is only false when $p$ is True and $q$ is False.
  • Converse: $q \rightarrow p$
  • Inverse: $\neg p \rightarrow \neg q$
  • Contrapositive: $\neg q \rightarrow \neg p$
Pro-Tip: A conditional statement is logically equivalent to its contrapositive! $p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg q \rightarrow \neg p$.
Having established the core principles of Negation, Converse, Inverse, and Contrapositive, we now turn our attention to Truth Tables and Classification.

Truth Tables and Classification

  • Tautology: A compound proposition that is always TRUE regardless of the truth values of its variables. Example: $p \vee \neg p$.
  • Contradiction: A compound proposition that is always FALSE. Example: $p \wedge \neg p$.
  • Contingency: A proposition that is neither a tautology nor a contradiction (has both T and F in its final column).
  • Biconditional ($p \leftrightarrow q$): True when both $p$ and $q$ have the same truth value. Example: '1+1=3 if and only if monkeys can fly' is TRUE because both are false!
  • Exclusive OR ($p \oplus q$): True when $p$ and $q$ have different truth values (one or the other, but not both).
Example: Checking $(p \rightarrow q) \vee (q \rightarrow p)$. Since one of $p \rightarrow q$ or $q \rightarrow p$ is always true for any T/F combination, the entire statement is a Tautology.
Having established the core principles of Truth Tables and Classification, we now turn our attention to Logical Equivalences & System Specifications.

Logical Equivalences & System Specifications

  • Logical Equivalence ($\equiv$): Two propositions are equivalent if they have the same truth table.
  • Important Laws:
    • Implication Law: $p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg p \vee q$
    • Negation of Implication: $\neg(p \rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q$
    • De Morgan's Laws: $\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$
  • Proving Equivalences: Can be done via Truth Tables (showing final columns match) or Logical Laws (applying rules step-by-step).
  • System Specifications: Translating rules into logic. Example: 'Access granted ($q$) whenever fee paid ($r$) and valid password entered ($p$)' translates to $(r \wedge p) \rightarrow q$.

Unit II: Recurrence Relations and Combinatorics

Permutations and Combinations

  • Permutations ($nP_r$): Ordered arrangements. Formula: $nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$. Example: Arranging 3 different books from 5 is $5P_3 = 60$.
  • Combinations ($nC_r$): Unordered selections. Formula: $nC_r = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$. Example: Selecting 3 students from 8 is $8C_3 = 56$.
  • Word Formations: 4-letter words from MATH is $4! = 24$.
  • Committees: Selecting 4 students from 6 boys and 5 girls means choosing 4 from 11 total: $11C_4$.
Exam Trap: Check if repetition is allowed or not! Default in distinct letter words is 'no repetition' ($nP_r$).
To fully appreciate Basics of Recurrence Relations, we must apply what we just learned about Permutations and Combinations.

Basics of Recurrence Relations

  • Recurrence Relation: An equation that recursively defines a sequence. Example: $a_n = 4a_{n-1} - a_{n-2}$.
  • Order: The difference between the highest and lowest subscripts. For $a_n = 6a_{n-1} - 9a_{n-2}$, order is $n - (n-2) = 2$.
  • Degree: The highest power of the terms. Linear means degree 1 (no $a_{n-1}^2$ or $a_{n-1}a_{n-2}$).
  • Finding Terms: Plug in previous values. For $a_n = a_{n-1}^2$ with $a_1=2$, $a_2 = 2^2 = 4$, $a_3 = 4^2 = 16$.
Building on our understanding of Basics of Recurrence Relations, let's dive into Solving Linear Recurrence Relations.

Solving Linear Recurrence Relations

  • Characteristic Equation: Replace $a_n$ with $r^n$. For $a_n - 5a_{n-1} + 6a_{n-2} = 0$, the equation is $r^2 - 5r + 6 = 0$.
  • Roots and Solutions:
    • Distinct Real Roots ($r_1 \neq r_2$): $a_n = A(r_1)^n + B(r_2)^n$
    • Repeated Roots ($r_1 = r_2$): $a_n = (A + Bn)(r_1)^n$
    • Degree 3 with roots $r_1, r_2, r_3$: $a_n = A(r_1)^n + B(r_2)^n + C(r_3)^n$
  • Finding Constants: Use initial conditions ($a_0, a_1$) to form a system of equations and solve for A, B, C.
Example: $a_n = 10a_{n-1} - 25a_{n-2} \implies r^2 - 10r + 25 = 0 \implies (r-5)^2 = 0$. Roots are 5, 5. Solution: $a_n = (A+Bn)5^n$.
To fully appreciate Generating Functions, we must apply what we just learned about Solving Linear Recurrence Relations.

Generating Functions

  • Definition: For a sequence $a_0, a_1, a_2...$, the generating function is $G(x) = a_0 + a_1x + a_2x^2 + ...$
  • Standard Sequence: $1, 2, 3, 4...$ corresponds to $G(x) = \frac{1}{(1-x)^2}$.
  • From Function to Sequence: $G(x) = \frac{1}{1-2x} = 1 + 2x + (2x)^2 + (2x)^3...$ corresponds to $a_n = 2^n$.
  • Solving Recurrences with GF:
    1. Multiply the recurrence by $x^n$ and sum from $n=1$ or $2$ to $\infty$.
    2. Substitute $G(x)$ for the sum.
    3. Solve algebraically for $G(x)$.
    4. Decompose into partial fractions and find the coefficient of $x^n$.

Unit III: Graph Theory

Graph Definitions and Types

  • Complete Graph ($K_n$): A simple graph where every pair of distinct vertices is connected by a unique edge.
  • Bipartite Graph: Vertices can be partitioned into two disjoint sets $V_1, V_2$ such that every edge connects a vertex in $V_1$ to one in $V_2$.
  • Complete Bipartite Graph ($K_{m,n}$): Every vertex in $V_1$ is connected to every vertex in $V_2$.
  • Cycles ($C_n$): A sequence of vertices connected in a closed loop.
  • Wheels ($W_n$): A cycle $C_n$ with an additional central vertex connected to all others.
  • n-Cube ($Q_n$): A graph where vertices represent bit strings of length $n$, and edges connect strings differing by exactly one bit.
  • Pseudograph: A graph that may contain multiple edges between the same vertices and self-loops.
To fully appreciate Graph Properties & Handshaking Theorem, we must apply what we just learned about Graph Definitions and Types.

Graph Properties & Handshaking Theorem

  • Degree of a Vertex ($deg(v)$): Number of edges incident to it. A loop contributes 2.
  • In-degree / Out-degree: In directed graphs, number of edges entering / leaving a vertex.
  • Isolated Vertex: Degree 0. Pendant Vertex: Degree 1.
  • Handshaking Theorem: The sum of degrees of all vertices equals TWICE the number of edges: $\sum deg(v) = 2|E|$.
Example: A graph has 15 edges, one vertex of deg 6, two of deg 5, three of deg 4, rest deg 1. Let $k$ be vertices of deg 1. Total degree = $2 \times 15 = 30$. Sum of degrees = $6 + (2\times 5) + (3\times 4) + k(1) = 28 + k$. So $28+k = 30 \implies k=2$. Total vertices = $1 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 8$.
Transitioning from Graph Properties & Handshaking Theorem, we now examine Graph Representations, which builds upon these foundational rules.

Graph Representations

  • Adjacency Matrix ($A$): A $V \times V$ matrix where $A_{ij} = 1$ if there is an edge between $v_i$ and $v_j$, else $0$. For undirected graphs, it is symmetric.
  • Incidence Matrix ($M$): A $V \times E$ matrix where $M_{ij} = 1$ if vertex $v_i$ is incident to edge $e_j$, else $0$. In directed graphs, usually $1$ for tail, $-1$ for head.
  • Drawing Graphs: Matrix representations can easily be converted back into visual graphs by mapping rows/cols to vertices/edges.
Now that we have a solid grasp of Graph Representations, the natural next step is to explore Graph Isomorphism & Bipartite Checking.

Graph Isomorphism & Bipartite Checking

  • Isomorphic Graphs: Two graphs $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic if there exists a one-to-one mapping between their vertices that preserves adjacency.
  • Invariants for Isomorphism: To check, ensure both graphs have: (1) Same number of vertices, (2) Same number of edges, (3) Same degree sequence. If any fail, they are NOT isomorphic.
  • Checking Bipartite: A graph is bipartite if and only if it does NOT contain any odd-length cycles. You can also try 2-coloring the vertices; if no two adjacent vertices share a color, it is bipartite.
Pro-Tip: If two graphs have the exact same invariants, they might be isomorphic, but you still have to verify the adjacency mapping. But if they differ, you can immediately say 'No'.
