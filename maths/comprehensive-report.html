(a) Prove: (p → q) ≡ (¬q → ¬p)
LHS = p → q
≡ ¬p ∨ q (Implication law)
≡ q ∨ ¬p (Commutative law)
≡ ¬(¬q) ∨ ¬p (Double negation)
≡ ¬q → ¬p (Implication law) = RHS. Hence Proved.

(b) Prove: ¬(p → q) ≡ p ∧ ¬q
LHS = ¬(p → q)
≡ ¬(¬p ∨ q) (Implication law)
≡ ¬(¬p) ∧ ¬q (De Morgan's law)
≡ p ∧ ¬q (Double negation) = RHS. Hence Proved.

Show that ¬(p ∨ (¬p ∧ q)) and ¬p ∧ ¬q are logically equivalent by developing a series of logical equivalences

We start with the LHS:

¬(p ∨ (¬p ∧ q))

≡ ¬p ∧ ¬(¬p ∧ q) (De Morgan's law)

≡ ¬p ∧ (¬(¬p) ∨ ¬q) (De Morgan's law)

≡ ¬p ∧ (p ∨ ¬q) (Double negation law)

≡ (¬p ∧ p) ∨ (¬p ∧ ¬q) (Distributive law)

≡ F ∨ (¬p ∧ ¬q) (Negation law)

≡ ¬p ∧ ¬q (Identity law)

This is exactly the RHS. Hence proved.

Following terms

(a) Tautology: A proposition that is always true. Ex: p ∨ ¬p.

(d) Contrapositive: Of p → q is ¬q → ¬p. They are logically equivalent.

(e) Exclusive OR: p ⊕ q. True when exactly one of p or q is true.
