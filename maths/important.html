Q24. State Eulers formula for a connected planar graph. Prove that Kuratowski’s graph other than K3,3 is non-planar.

Unit IV CO4 | Bloom L4 5 Marks End Term Score: 9.7

1. Statement of Euler's Formula:

For any connected planar graph $G$ drawn in the plane with $v$ vertices, $e$ edges, and $r$ regions (faces, including the outer unbounded face):

$$\\nu - e + r = 2$$

2. Kuratowski's Graphs:

Kuratowski's Theorem states that a graph is planar if and only if it does not contain a subgraph that is homeomorphic to $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$. The Kuratowski graph other than $K_{3,3}$ is the complete graph $K_5$.

3. Proof that $K_5$ is Non-Planar:

Step 1: Edge-Face Inequality.
In any simple planar graph with $v \ge 3$, every region is bounded by a cycle of length at least 3 edges. Furthermore, each edge separates at most two regions (or borders one region twice). Let $B_i$ denote the number of boundary edges of region $i$. Then:

$$\\sum_{i=1}^r B_i = 2e$$

Since $B_i \ge 3$ for each of the $r$ regions:

$$2e = \\sum_{i=1}^r B_i \ge 3r \implies r \le \\frac{2}{3}e$$

Step 2: Combining with Euler's Formula.
Substitute $r = 2 - v + e$ into the inequality:

$$2 - v + e \le \\frac{2}{3}e \implies 2 - v \le -\\frac{1}{3}e \implies \\frac{1}{3}e \le v - 2 \implies e \le 3v - 6$$

Hence, any simple connected planar graph must satisfy $e \le 3v - 6$.

Step 3: Evaluating for $K_5$.
For $K_5$:

  • Number of vertices: $v = 5$
  • Number of edges: $e = \\binom{5}{2} = \\frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 10$

Testing the planar inequality:

$$3v - 6 = 3(5) - 6 = 15 - 6 = 9$$

Since $e = 10 > 9$, $K_5$ violates the fundamental planar inequality $e \le 3v - 6$. Therefore, $K_5$ cannot be embedded in the plane without edges crossing, proving that $K_5$ is non-planar.

  • Euler's Formula: $v - e + r = 2$ for all connected planar graphs.
  • Kuratowski's Other Graph: The complete graph $K_5$ (5 vertices, 10 edges).
  • The Proof:
    • Each region needs at least 3 border edges $\implies 2e \ge 3r$.
    • Plugging into Euler gives $e \le 3v - 6$.
    • For $K_5$: $v=5 \implies 3(5) - 6 = 9$. But $K_5$ has 10 edges!
    • $10 > 9$ breaks the law $\implies K_5$ is non-planar.

Q56. Answer the following: (i) Prove that (S30, D) is a lattice, where S30 = {1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30}. (ii) The Hasse diagram of a poset is shown below. Is it a lattice? Justify. a b c d 3

Unit V CO5 | Bloom L4 5 Marks End Term Score: 9.7

(i) Prove that $(S_{30}, D)$ is a lattice:

The set $S_{30} = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30\}$ is the set of all divisors of $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$. To prove it is a lattice, we show that every pair $x, y \in S_{30}$ has a unique greatest lower bound (glb) and least upper bound (lub) inside $S_{30}$:

  1. Greatest Lower Bound (Meet):
    For any $x, y \in S_{30}$, $\text{glb}(x, y) = \gcd(x, y)$. Since $x \mid 30$, any divisor of $x$ also divides 30. Thus $\gcd(x, y) \mid 30$, which guarantees $\gcd(x, y) \in S_{30}$. By the properties of gcd, it is the unique greatest lower bound.
  2. Least Upper Bound (Join):
    For any $x, y \in S_{30}$, $\text{lub}(x, y) = \text{lcm}(x, y)$. Because both $x \mid 30$ and $y \mid 30$, 30 is a common multiple of $x$ and $y$. By definition of least common multiple, $\text{lcm}(x, y)$ divides every common multiple of $x$ and $y$, which implies $\text{lcm}(x, y) \mid 30$. Hence $\text{lcm}(x, y) \in S_{30}$. By properties of lcm, it is the unique least upper bound.

Because $S_{30}$ is closed under $\gcd$ and $\text{lcm}$, every pair has a unique meet and join. Thus $(S_{30}, D)$ is a lattice (isomorphic to the Boolean lattice $B_3$).

(ii) Analysis of the Given Hasse Diagram:

The diagram shows four elements $\{a, b, c, d\}$ where $a \prec c, a \prec d, b \prec c, b \prec d$.

  • Consider the pair of elements $\{a, b\}$ at the bottom level.
  • The set of upper bounds of $\{a, b\}$ is $\{c, d\}$ (since $a \le c, b \le c$ and $a \le d, b \le d$).
  • For a least upper bound (lub) to exist, there must be a unique least element in the set of upper bounds $\{c, d\}$.
  • However, $c$ and $d$ are non-comparable ($c \not\le d$ and $d \not\le c$). Neither $c \le d$ nor $d \le c$ holds.
  • Therefore, $\text{lub}(a, b)$ does NOT exist. (Similarly, $\text{glb}(c, d)$ does not exist).

Because least upper bounds fail to exist for the pair $\{a, b\}$, the poset is NOT a lattice.

  • (i) $(S_{30}, D)$ is a Lattice: Both $\gcd$ and $ ext{lcm}$ of any two divisors of 30 divide 30, so they stay inside $S_{30}$. Every pair has unique meet and join.
  • (ii) Crown Poset is NOT a Lattice:
    • Upper bounds of $a$ and $b$ are $\{c, d\}$.
    • Neither $c$ nor $d$ is smaller than the other.
    • No *least* upper bound exists $\implies$ Fails lattice condition!

Q20. A town has four land masses A, B,C, D connected by seven bridges: two bridges between A and B, two between A and C, and one each between A and D, B and D, C and D. (i) Model the situation as a multigraph and draw it. (ii) Find the degree of each vertex. (iii) Does the multigraph have an Euler circuit or an Euler path? Justify. (iv) If one of the bridges between A and B is closed, show that an Euler path exists and write it. 3

Unit IV CO4 | Bloom L3 5 Marks End Term Score: 9.6

This problem is a classic variant of the Königsberg Bridge Problem modeled using graph theory.

(i) Multigraph Model:

Vertices represent the four land masses $V = \{A, B, C, D\}$, and edges represent the 7 bridges: parallel edges $e_1, e_2$ between $A$ and $B$; parallel edges $e_3, e_4$ between $A$ and $C$; single edges $e_5 = (A, D), e_6 = (B, D), e_7 = (C, D)$.

(ii) Degree of each vertex:

  • $\\deg(A) = 2 (\text{to } B) + 2 (\text{to } C) + 1 (\text{to } D) = 5$
  • $\\deg(B) = 2 (\text{to } A) + 1 (\text{to } D) = 3$
  • $\\deg(C) = 2 (\text{to } A) + 1 (\text{to } D) = 3$
  • $\\deg(D) = 1 (\text{to } A) + 1 (\text{to } B) + 1 (\text{to } C) = 3$

Sum of degrees $= 5 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 14 = 2 \times 7$ edges (verifying Handshaking Lemma).

(iii) Does the multigraph have an Euler circuit or an Euler path?

By Euler's Theorem, a connected multigraph has an Euler circuit if all vertices have even degree, and an Euler path if exactly 0 or 2 vertices have odd degree. Here, all four vertices ($A, B, C, D$) have odd degrees. Since the number of odd-degree vertices is 4 (exceeding 2), the multigraph has neither an Euler circuit nor an Euler path.

(iv) If one bridge between $A$ and $B$ is closed:

Closing one bridge between $A$ and $B$ removes one edge between $A$ and $B$. The new vertex degrees are:

  • $\\deg'(A) = 5 - 1 = 4$ (even)
  • $\\deg'(B) = 3 - 1 = 2$ (even)
  • $\\deg'(C) = 3$ (odd)
  • $\\deg'(D) = 3$ (odd)

Now, exactly two vertices ($C$ and $D$) have odd degrees, while $A$ and $B$ have even degrees. The graph remains connected. Therefore, by Euler's Theorem, an Euler path exists starting at $C$ and ending at $D$ (or vice versa).

A valid Euler path traversing all 6 remaining bridges is:

$$C \xrightarrow{\text{bridge 1}} A \xrightarrow{\text{remaining bridge}} B \to D \to A \xrightarrow{\text{bridge 2}} C \to D$$

Every one of the 6 bridges is crossed exactly once.

  • Model: 4 nodes, 7 bridges. Degrees are $A: 5, B: 3, C: 3, D: 3$.
  • Original State: 4 odd nodes $\implies$ Impossible to cross all bridges once without repeating (no Euler path/circuit).
  • Removing one $A-B$ bridge: Degrees become $A: 4, B: 2, C: 3, D: 3$. Exactly two odd nodes ($C$ and $D$) remain!
  • Euler Path: Can now traverse all bridges starting at $C$ and ending at $D$.

Q10. State the Four Color Theorem. Using it, explain why K5 is not planar.

Unit IV CO4 | Bloom L2 2 Marks End Term Score: 9.5

Four Color Theorem: Every planar graph can be properly colored using at most 4 colors ($\\chi(G) \le 4$).

Why $K_5$ is not planar:

In the complete graph $K_5$, all 5 vertices are mutually adjacent. Therefore, each vertex must receive a distinct color, which gives $\\chi(K_5) = 5$. If $K_5$ were planar, by the Four Color Theorem, its chromatic number would be at most 4. Since $\\chi(K_5) = 5 > 4$, by contraposition, $K_5$ cannot be planar.

  • Theorem: Any map or planar graph needs at most 4 colors.
  • $K_5$ check: $K_5$ has 5 vertices all joined together, requiring 5 colors.
  • Conclusion: Since $5 > 4$, $K_5$ cannot be planar!

Q23. A connected planar simple graph has 9 vertices with degrees 4 , 4, 4, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2. (a) Find the number of edges and the number of regions. V erify that e ≤ 3v − 6. (b) Show that K6 is not planar. (c) Show that every connected planar simple graph with at least 3 vertices has a vertex of degree at most 5.

Unit IV CO4 | Bloom L4 5 Marks End Term Score: 9.5

(a) Number of edges and regions, and verification of $e \le 3v - 6$:

  1. Edges ($e$): By the Handshaking Lemma, the sum of degrees equals twice the number of edges:
    $$\\sum_{v \in V} \\deg(v) = 2e$$$$\\sum \\deg(v) = (3 \times 4) + (4 \times 3) + (2 \times 2) = 12 + 12 + 4 = 28$$$$2e = 28 \implies e = 14$$
  2. Regions ($r$): Using Euler's formula $v - e + r = 2$ for connected planar graphs with $v = 9$ and $e = 14$:
    $$9 - 14 + r = 2 \implies -5 + r = 2 \implies r = 7$$The graph divides the plane into 7 regions.
  3. Verifying $e \le 3v - 6$:
    $$3v - 6 = 3(9) - 6 = 27 - 6 = 21$$Since $e = 14 \le 21$, the inequality is satisfied.

(b) Show that $K_6$ is not planar:

The complete graph $K_6$ has $v = 6$ vertices and $e = \\binom{6}{2} = \\frac{6 \times 5}{2} = 15$ edges. If $K_6$ were planar, it would satisfy the necessary condition $e \le 3v - 6$ for simple planar graphs:

$$3v - 6 = 3(6) - 6 = 18 - 6 = 12$$

However, $e = 15 > 12$. Because $K_6$ exceeds the maximum possible number of edges for a planar graph of 6 vertices, $K_6$ cannot be planar.

(c) Prove that every connected planar simple graph ($v \ge 3$) has a vertex of degree at most 5:

We prove this by contradiction. Suppose there exists a connected planar simple graph $G$ with $v \ge 3$ vertices in which every vertex has degree at least 6 ($\\deg(v) \ge 6$ for all $v \in V$).

Summing over all degrees:

$$2e = \\sum_{v \in V} \\deg(v) \ge 6v \implies e \ge 3v$$

However, for any planar simple graph with $v \ge 3$, we have:

$$e \le 3v - 6$$

Combining both inequalities:

$$3v \le e \le 3v - 6 \implies 3v \le 3v - 6 \implies 0 \le -6$$

This is an obvious contradiction. Therefore, our initial assumption is false, and every connected planar simple graph with $v \ge 3$ must have at least one vertex of degree $\\le 5$.
